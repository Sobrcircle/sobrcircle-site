Evicting a tenant in Ontario: the process, step by step.

Evicting a tenant is a legal process under the Residential Tenancies Act, decided by the Landlord and Tenant Board. You need a reason the law allows, the matching notice served the right way, an application, a hearing and an order. Only the Sheriff can carry out the eviction. The Board sets the pace, and a mistake in the notice or its service starts the clock again.

Start here

No valid reason, no eviction.

Ontario is a for-cause province: you can’t end a tenancy just because you want the unit back. A fixed-term lease continues month to month when it ends, so the end of a lease is not a reason on its own. Each reason has its own notice and its own application.

Which notice and application go with each reason for ending a tenancy
ReasonNoticeMinimum noticeCan the tenant fix it?Then apply
Rent not paidN4At least 7 daysYes: paying everything owing voids itL1
Damage, disturbing others or overcrowdingN520 days on a first N5Yes: 7 days to correct, first N5 onlyL2
An illegal act in the unitN6Set by the form (see the forms guide)NoL2
Serious damage or a safety riskN710 daysNoL2
Rent paid late, again and againN860 days, ending on the last day of a rental periodNoL2
You, a buyer or an eligible family member will move inN1260 days, ending on the last day of a rental periodNoL2, which can be filed once the notice is served
Demolition, conversion or major repairsN13120 days, ending on the last day of a rental periodNoL2, which can be filed once the notice is served

Notice periods are minimums for notices served on or after September 21, 2026. For an N4, don’t count the day you serve, and add 5 days if you mail it or 1 if you send it by courier. An own-use N12 with at least 120 days’ notice needs no compensation; with less, or for a buyer, one month’s compensation applies. N13 compensation and the tenant’s right to return depend on the work and the building. Each form, its deadlines and its version are in the LTB forms guide; what changed in 2026 is in the Bill 60 guide.

The process

Six steps, in order.

Each step has to be done correctly before the next. An error at any stage, especially the notice, can send you back to the start.

  1. 1

    Identify the legal ground

    Match what is happening to a reason the Residential Tenancies Act allows. If there is no valid ground, there is no eviction.

  2. 2

    Serve the right notice, then wait

    The right N form, filled out correctly, delivered by a valid method, with a Certificate of Service. You can’t apply to the Board before the notice period ends.

  3. 3

    File the application

    If the tenant hasn’t paid or corrected the problem, file the matching application: an L1 for unpaid rent, an L2 for most other grounds.

  4. 4

    Prepare your evidence

    A clean rent ledger, photos, written messages and witnesses, organised the way adjudicators expect. Missing evidence gets hearings adjourned.

  5. 5

    Attend the hearing

    Usually by video or phone. The tenant can raise defences or ask for more time. The adjudicator decides and issues a written order.

  6. 6

    Enforce the order

    If the tenant stays past the date in the order, file it with the Court Enforcement Office. Only the Sheriff can carry out an eviction.

Preparing for your hearing

Joining

Your link, meeting ID or dial-in number is on your own Notice of Hearing and is unique to your case. Join early and test your sound and video. We don’t publish hearing links, and you should never rely on a link from anyone other than the Board.

What to have ready

Your Notice of Hearing, your application and the notice you served, your Certificate of Service, your rent ledger and evidence, and photo ID. Keep digital copies open and ready to share.

On the day

Stay muted until your case is called, use a quiet space, and let one person speak at a time. If you miss the hearing, the Board may go ahead without you or dismiss your application.

Can’t find your Notice of Hearing or your link? Contact the Landlord and Tenant Board before the hearing date. Our licensed paralegals attend hearings for clients.

How long it takes

The Board sets the pace.

Nobody can promise you a date, and we won’t. Every case moves through the same five stages. The notice period and the Board’s schedule are fixed; most other delays come from a file that has to start again.

The five stages of an eviction and what sets the length of each
StageWhat sets the lengthWhat happens
1.Notice periodSet by the notice: 7 days for an N4, 60 for an N8 or N12, 120 for an N13The tenant can pay or correct, where the notice allows it, and the notice is void.
2.Filing with the BoardAs soon as the notice period ends (sooner for an N12 or N13)You file the L1 or L2 with your evidence and pay the Board’s filing fee.
3.Waiting for a hearingSet by the Board’s schedule. It varies, and it is the least predictable stageThe Board sends both sides a Notice of Hearing with the date, time and how to join.
4.Hearing and orderThe order follows the hearingThe adjudicator hears both sides and issues a written order with the date the tenant must leave.
5.Sheriff enforcementOnly if the tenant stays past the date in the orderYou file the order with the Court Enforcement Office, which schedules the Sheriff.

Outside your control

The notice period, the Board’s schedule, the tenant raising defences or asking for a payment plan, and the Sheriff’s calendar.

Avoidable

A defective notice or bad service (start over), missing or disorganised evidence (adjournment), filing late, and waiting to file with the Court Enforcement Office after the order.

Own-use and renovation cases start longer, because the notice period alone is 60 to 120 days. Contested cases take longer again. No timeline is guaranteed.

Avoid these

The mistakes that restart the clock.

A defective notice or bad service means serving again and starting over. Avoiding that one kind of mistake saves the most time.

  • Serving the wrong notice

    An N5 for unpaid rent, or an N12 that doesn’t meet its strict good-faith and compensation rules, gets the application dismissed. So does an old version of the form.

  • Invalid service

    A text message, email without the tenant’s consent, or a notice taped to the door is not valid service. Complete a Certificate of Service every time.

  • Filing too early or too late

    File before the notice period ends and the application is rejected. Wait too long and you lose rent, and some applications have deadlines.

  • Doing it yourself, physically

    Changing the locks, removing belongings or cutting off utilities is illegal, even with an order in hand. It can turn your case into a claim against you.

The Board reissued the N4 and ten other forms in September 2026. Check the version on the LTB’s site before you serve or file. Form by form, the LTB forms guide lists what voids each one.

Questions.

Does the tenant have to leave when the lease ends?

No. In Ontario a fixed-term lease continues month to month when it ends. To end the tenancy you still need a reason the law allows, the matching notice and, if the tenant stays, an order from the Landlord and Tenant Board.

How long does an eviction take?

It varies, and the Board sets the schedule. The notice period is the minimum before you can apply, then you wait for a hearing date, then for the order. Contested cases take longer. The delay you can control is a restart: a defective notice or invalid service means serving again and starting the clock over.

Can I change the locks or remove the tenant’s belongings?

No. Changing the locks, removing belongings or shutting off utilities to force a tenant out is illegal in Ontario, even after the Board has issued an order. Only the Sheriff (the Court Enforcement Office) can carry out an eviction.

What if the tenant pays after I serve an N4?

If the tenant pays everything owing before the termination date on the N4, the notice is void. A partial payment, or a payment after the L1 is filed, is different: the case can often continue, but every amount has to be tracked exactly.

Can I charge a late fee?

No. Ontario landlords can’t charge late fees or penalties for late rent, even if the lease says so, and only unpaid rent goes on an N4. The remedies for late rent are the N4 and, for a pattern of lateness, the N8.

Can I do this without a paralegal?

Yes, you can represent yourself at the Board. The process is strict: one error in the notice, its service or your evidence can mean starting over. Our licensed paralegals can review your file or take it on at any stage.

Are hearings in person?

Most Board hearings are held by video or by phone. Your Notice of Hearing gives the format, your time and how to join; the link is unique to your case. Never rely on a hearing link from anyone other than the Board.

What if I miss my hearing?

The Board may go ahead without you or dismiss your application. Join early, test your sound and video, and contact the Board straight away if something goes wrong. A licensed paralegal can attend for you.

What happens after the order?

The Board issues a written order with the date the tenant must leave. If the tenant stays past that date, you file the order with the Court Enforcement Office and the Sheriff carries out the eviction. A request to review an order has to be made within 15 days of the order; the Bill 60 guide covers that change.

Does a paralegal make it faster?

A paralegal can’t shorten the notice periods or the Board’s schedule. What they remove are the delays you control: the right notice served the right way the first time, filing as soon as the notice period ends, and complete evidence so the hearing isn’t adjourned.
